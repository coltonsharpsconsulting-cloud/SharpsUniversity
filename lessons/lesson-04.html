Types of Angles

Hi Stephen — Lesson 4. Last lesson you learned to put an exact number on an angle. This one is about what you call it once you have that number, and about the two measures — 90° and 180° — that are important enough to get their own names instead of being lumped in with the rest.

1 · Read first · ~10 min
Five names, and every one of them is decided purely by the number of degrees. No judgement, no “looks about right” — measure first, then name.

The five types

Acute — more than 0° and less than 90°. A tight opening.
Right — exactly 90°. A square corner.
Obtuse — more than 90° and less than 180°. Wider than square, not yet flat.
Straight — exactly 180°. Two opposite rays forming one line.
Reflex — more than 180° and less than 360°. The angle that goes the long way round.

Notice the boundaries are exclusive. 90° is not acute and not obtuse — it's right, full stop. The two exact values get their own names because so much of geometry depends on them being exact.

Why 90° and 180° get their own words

Everything else is a range; these two are single values. A right angle is what perpendicular means, what a square corner is, what the Pythagorean theorem requires. A straight angle is the whole idea of a line. Calling 90° “a small obtuse angle” would blur the one measurement the rest of the subject is built on — so it gets its own name and its own mark.

That's also why a right angle is drawn with a small square, never an arc. The mark says “this is exactly 90° by construction” — you're not meant to measure it.

Reflex is the one people miss

Two rays at a vertex actually make two angles: the one you naturally see, and the one wrapping the other way around. Together they complete a full turn, so they always add to 360°.

If the inner angle is 110°, the reflex angle at that same vertex is 360 − 110 = 250°. Both are real, and both describe the same pair of rays — you have to say which one you mean.

A clock is the easiest place to see it. At 4 o'clock the hands make 120° the short way and 240° the long way. Same hands, same vertex, two legitimate answers.

Adding types

You can often predict the type of a sum without knowing the exact numbers:

• right + acute → always obtuse (90 plus something under 90 lands between 90 and 180)
• right + right → always straight (90 + 90 = 180)
• obtuse + obtuse → always reflex (each is over 90, so the sum clears 180)
• acute + acute → could be anything up to obtuse — 20 + 30 = 50 is acute, but 80 + 80 = 160 is obtuse.

That last one is worth sitting with: two tight angles can add up to a wide one.

The sum is more than 90 and less than 180 no matter which acute angle you pick, so it's always obtuse. True.

04Reasoning from ranges
Two obtuse angles can add up to a straight angle.
Hint
What's the smallest an obtuse angle can be? Add two of those together.
Check my answer

Answer: False

Each obtuse angle is already over 90°, so two of them clear 180° between them.

Both are greater than 90°, so their sum must be greater than 180° — past straight, into reflex. It can never be exactly 180°. False.
⚑ Two right angles do make a straight angle. It's the strictly-greater-than that rules obtuse out.
05The two angles at every vertex
At one vertex, an angle and its reflex partner add to 360°.
Hint
If you go around the inner opening and then around the outside, how far have you turned in total?
Check my answer

Answer: True

The reflex angle is the same pair of rays measured the long way round — together they complete a full turn.

The two together sweep one complete revolution, which is 360°. So a 110° angle has a 250° reflex partner. True.
06The sum with no guaranteed type
Two acute angles always add to another acute angle.
Hint
Try two small ones, then try two large ones. Do you get the same type both times?
Check my answer

Answer: False

20° + 30° = 50°, still acute. But 80° + 80° = 160°, which is obtuse.

Small acute angles add to an acute angle, but two large ones can pass 90° — 80 + 80 = 160°, obtuse. Acute + acute is the sum with no guaranteed type. False.
⚑ It can even reach exactly 90°: 45 + 45. So acute + acute can be acute, right, or obtuse.

12
The hands of a clock at 4:00 make an angle. Give both measures and name each type.
Show me a good answer
Each hour is 30°, so four hours is 120° the short way — obtuse. The long way round is 360 − 120 = 240° — reflex. Both describe the same two hands; you have to say which you mean.
13
Why does a right angle get drawn with a small square instead of an arc with “90°” written on it?
Show me a good answer
The square declares it is exactly 90° by construction — a fact of the figure rather than a measurement someone took. So much of geometry (perpendicular lines, the Pythagorean theorem) depends on that exactness that it gets a mark of its own, and you're not meant to measure it.
14
Your friend says “two acute angles always make an acute angle.” Give one example that supports them and one that proves them wrong.
Show me a good answer
Supports: 20° + 30° = 50°, still acute. Disproves: 80° + 80° = 160°, obtuse. One counterexample is enough to sink an “always” claim — and this is the sum with no guaranteed type.
