Points, Lines, Rays & Segments

Hi Stephen — Lesson 1, and the start of everything. Four figures, and the whole lesson is really one question: where does it stop? That single distinction decides what each one is called, how you write it, and whether it has a length at all.

1 · Read first · ~10 min
A point marks a position and has no size. Everything else in geometry is built from points — the difference between the figures is simply how far they run before they stop.

Where does it stop?

Point — a single exact position. No length, no width. Named with one capital letter: P.

Segment — stops at both ends. Written AB with a plain bar over it, and it's the only one of the four with a length you can measure.

Ray — stops at one end and runs forever the other way. Written AB with a one-way arrow: it starts at A and goes through B forever.

Line — never stops. Written AB with a two-way arrow.

Arrowheads aren't decoration. They're how a reader sees that something doesn't end. Leave them off a line and you've drawn a segment.

The first letter of a ray is the endpoint

This is the rule that catches people. In ray AB, A is where it starts and B just tells you which direction it heads. So ray AB and ray BA are different rays — they start at opposite ends and point opposite ways.

Compare that with segments and lines, where the order doesn't matter at all: segment AB is the same as segment BA, and line AB is the same as line BA.

If you remember one thing from this lesson, make it this. Ray order matters. Segment and line order does not.

Collinear just means "on the same line"

Points are collinear if one straight line passes through all of them. Any two points are automatically collinear — you can always draw a line through two points. It only becomes an interesting question with three or more.

A segment has a length. A ray and a line don't.

AB (with the bar) is the segment — a figure. AB with no bar is its length — a number. So you write AB = 5 cm, not segment AB = 5 cm.

Ask a ray how long it is and there's no answer. Not "very long" — no answer, because there's no second end to measure to. Infinite isn't a big number; it's the absence of an endpoint.

Same distinction turns up again in Lesson 3 with angles: ∠ABC is the figure, m∠ABC is the number. Geometry keeps the shape and its size in separate notation on purpose.

Part of a line

Every segment and every ray sits on some line, and is a piece of it. If segment AB and ray AB both lie along the same straight path as line BC, then both are included in line BC. A smaller piece of a bigger thing — nothing more mysterious than that.

Words to own

segmentStops at both ends. Has a measurable length.

In your own words

12
Explain, in your own words, why a ray has no measurable length — without using the word “infinite.”
Show me a good answer
A ray only stops at one end. To measure something you need two places to put the ends of your ruler, and a ray never gives you the second one. It isn't that the number is too big to write — there's no second endpoint to measure to, so no measurement exists.
13
Your friend writes “ray AB and ray BA are the same, just written backwards.” Correct them, and say which figures they'd be right about.
Show me a good answer
Rays are named endpoint-first, so ray AB starts at A and ray BA starts at B — opposite directions, different figures. They would be right about segments and lines: segment AB = segment BA, and line AB = line BA, because those have no direction to reverse.
